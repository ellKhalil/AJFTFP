Abstract

This study examines the effect of firm-specific attributes on financial reporting quality (FRQ) among quoted oil marketing companies in Nigeria. Anchored on Agency Theory and Positive Accounting Theory, this study conceptualises firm attributes as governance-related mechanisms that influence managerial reporting incentives and monitoring intensity. Using panel data from 11 firms over the period 2010–2023, the study employs a fixed-effects estimation technique, following Hausman specification tests, to control for unobserved heterogeneity. FRQ is proxied by the audit report lag, reflecting reporting timeliness as a key qualitative characteristic. The explanatory variables include firm size, leverage, firm age, and profitability (ROA). The empirical findings reveal that firm size, leverage, and profitability exert a positive and statistically significant influence on financial reporting quality, while firm age exhibits an insignificant effect. The results suggest that larger and more profitable firms, as well as firms with higher leverage, are subject to stronger monitoring and market discipline, thereby enhancing reporting quality. The study concludes that firm attributes have a significant influence on the FRQ of oil companies in Nigeria. Therefore, it is recommended that oil companies in Nigeria implement strategic capital structure decisions, optimise operational efficiency, and increase shareholder value to attain sustainable growth. This study contributes to the literature by providing robust panel-based evidence from the Nigerian oil sector and by reconciling mixed findings in prior studies through a governance-oriented perspective. The findings have implications for regulators, investors, and corporate managers in designing mechanisms that enhance FRQ in emerging economies.

Keywords: Firm Attributes, Financial Reporting Quality, Sustainable Growth, Audit Report Lag, Oil Marketing Companies, Nigeria

  • INTRODUCTION

Financial reporting quality (FRQ) remains a central concern in accounting and corporate governance research, given its critical role in reducing information asymmetry and enhancing the efficiency of capital markets. High-quality financial reports provide relevant, reliable, and timely information that enables investors, creditors, and other stakeholders to make informed economic decisions. However, the quality of financial reporting is not solely a function of accounting standards and regulatory frameworks; it is also shaped by firm-specific characteristics that influence managerial incentives, monitoring intensity, and reporting behaviour (Ayuba, Bitrus & Mahmud, 2024). The paradigm shift from a conventional management and reporting approach to a more sophisticated, holistic, and scientific approach was necessitated by the realisation that the conflict of interest arises as a result of the separation of ownership from management. The division between ownership and control, along with the owners' lack of experience in business and financial affairs, will persist in creating challenges in organisational management unless a robust system of internal and external controls is established to safeguard the interests of diverse stakeholders (Muhammad & Abubakar, 2025).

Financial reporting quality has four main dimensions: disclosure, relevance, reliability, and timeliness. These dimensions describe various aspects of quality that a financial report is expected to exhibit. The quality of a financial report could be judged by how much information is disclosed therein. Disclosure of financial reports and the extent of what is disclosed in the report are determined by both statute (mandatory disclosure) and voluntary disclosure by reporting entities. Voluntary disclosure brings to the public domain both quantitative and qualitative information about an entity's operations, without any legal compulsion (Aniefor et al., 2021). This helps reduce information asymmetry and could be an indicator of management's transparency and accountability in its stewardship (Madi et al., 2014). The reliability dimension of financial reporting quality is another fundamental feature that reflects the faithful representation of information in the report. The representation of the information must be reliably complete, in the sense that all necessary information for decision-making by users is accurately depicted. It must also be reliably neutral, ensuring that there is no bias in the selection and presentation of financial information, and it must be reliably free from errors and omissions in its information content and in the process used in producing the information. The relevance feature of quality financial reporting, one of its

fundamental characteristics, describes and emphasises the usefulness and materiality of financial reports. When a financial report can induce and facilitate decision-making by users, it is said to possess the relevance quality. The relevance of a financial report is affected by its materiality. The information content of the financial report must be sufficiently adequate to address the user's needs for decision-making.

This study focuses on the reliability aspect of financial reporting quality, specifically examining the reliability of reported earnings, utilising Audit Report Lag as a proxy for FRQ. This dimension is selected for this study due to its fundamental role in financial reports and their utility. If a financial report's content is relevant, adequately disclosed, and timely, yet fails to represent its subject matter accurately, any predictions or decisions derived from it will be flawed. Earnings are a rallying point of interest for various stakeholders of corporations; hence, what is reported as earnings must be true, reflecting the economic realities of transactions and free of any opportunistic manipulation by management. Research indicates that the accounting earnings disclosed by corporations often lack reliability and effectiveness, potentially contributing to the global financial crisis. Regulatory agencies and stakeholders prioritise the authenticity of financial reports significantly. The accuracy of the report is contingent upon the dependability of the reported earnings. Management's manipulation of earnings undermines the quality of financial reports and reduces investor confidence (Bello et al., 2024). 

In emerging economies such as Nigeria, where institutional enforcement mechanisms are relatively weak and market imperfections persist, firm attributes play an even more pronounced role in determining FRQ. The oil sector, in particular, presents a compelling context for analysis due to its economic significance, regulatory scrutiny, and exposure to complex financial and operational risks. These features heighten the need for credible and transparent financial reporting while simultaneously creating incentives for opportunistic reporting behaviour. Prior empirical studies have examined the relationship between firm attributes, such as firm size, leverage, profitability, and age and FRQ, but the findings remain mixed and inconclusive. While some studies suggest that larger firms and highly leveraged firms produce higher-quality financial reports due to increased monitoring and political visibility, others report insignificant or even negative relationships. These inconsistencies may be attributed to differences in measurement approaches, model specifications, and institutional contexts. Furthermore, much of the existing literature in Nigeria has relied on cross-sectional designs or limited econometric controls, thereby constraining the robustness and generalisability of findings.

Attributes specific to firms, including size, age, and financial structure, significantly influence the quality of financial reporting among Nigerian oil and gas companies. Some attributes, including profitability and liquidity, generally have a positive effect, while firm size can have a negative impact on these factors. Leverage, firm age, and ownership structure also show varying and often negative effects depending on the specific attribute and quality measure. 

This study addresses these gaps by providing a comprehensive panel-data analysis of the nexus between firm attributes and FRQ among quoted oil marketing companies in Nigeria. Specifically, the study investigates the extent to which firm size, leverage, firm age, and profitability influence FRQ, proxied by audit report lag. By employing fixed-effects estimation, the study controls for unobserved firm-specific heterogeneity, thereby improving the reliability of the results. This study provides updated empirical evidence on FRQ within the Nigerian oil sector using a longitudinal panel design. The study further integrates theoretical insights from Agency Theory and Positive Accounting Theory to offer a more coherent explanation of the relationship between firm attributes and reporting quality. Enhances methodological rigour by employing robust panel estimation techniques, thereby addressing limitations in prior studies.

Given the foregoing, this paper attempts to bridge the existing gaps in the effect of firm-specific attributes on the FRQ of oil marketing companies in Nigeria.


II. LITERATURE REVIEW AND THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK

  • Conceptual Review

This section reviews relevant and related concepts regarding firm-specific attributes and the financial reporting quality of oil and gas companies.

2.1 Concept of Financial Reporting Quality 

Financial reporting quality (FRQ) refers to the extent to which financial statements and reports adhere to established standards regarding detail completeness, decision-making usefulness, intent credibility, and presentation timeliness. The concept is interpreted variably. Shehu (2023) indicates that the literature lacks a consistent and widely accepted definition of FRQ. Financial reporting quality (FRQ) is a multidimensional construct that captures the extent to which financial statements faithfully represent a firm’s underlying economic performance and position while remaining useful for decision-making. Prior literature (Martinez-Ferrero, 2014; Muhammad & Abubakar, 2025) conceptualises FRQ along key qualitative dimensions, including relevance, reliability (faithful representation), comparability, and timeliness. However, empirical studies often operationalise FRQ using single proxies, such as discretionary accruals, earnings persistence, or audit report lag; each capturing distinct but partial aspects of reporting quality (Abdulsalam & Babangida, (2022). This divergence in measurement has contributed to inconsistencies in empirical findings. For instance, accrual-based measures emphasise earnings manipulation and managerial discretion, whereas timeliness-based measures (e.g., audit report lag) reflect reporting efficiency and governance effectiveness. Consequently, the choice of proxy is not merely methodological but also theoretical, as it determines the dimension of reporting quality being examined. This study adopts audit report lag as a proxy for FRQ, aligning with the timeliness dimension and reflecting the efficiency of financial reporting processes and the intensity of external monitoring.

2.2 Empirical Review and Hypotheses Development 

This section discusses the empirical works of previous studies and develops Hypotheses that guide the study.

2.2.1 Firm Size and Financial Reporting Quality 

Empirical evidence on the relationship between firm size and FRQ remains mixed but exhibits a discernible pattern when contextualised within monitoring and political cost frameworks. A stream of studies finds that larger firms exhibit higher reporting quality, attributed to stronger internal controls, greater regulatory scrutiny, and more sophisticated governance structures. Larger firms also face higher reputational costs, which may deter opportunistic reporting. Conversely, other studies report insignificant or even negative relationships, suggesting that firm size may also provide greater opportunities for earnings management due to organisational complexity and information opacity. Muhammad and Abubakar (2025) investigated whether specific board attributes are systematically associated with financial reporting quality (FRQ) in Nigerian-listed oil and gas companies over 13 years (2012–2024). Guided by Agency Theory and Resource Dependence Theory, the analysis employed multivariate panel techniques suitable for firm-level data observed over time, with standard controls for scale effects and unobserved heterogeneity. The results indicate that firm size is negatively and significantly related to discretionary accruals, suggesting that larger issuers have stronger information environments and internal controls. The study recommends, among other things, that Oil and Gas firms should embed independence first in nomination policies; adopt rigorous conflict-of-interest and tenure-refresh practices; and periodically rotate committee membership to sustain challenge. It is also recommended that oil and gas firms in Nigeria should recruit and retain industry/financial experts on their boards. 

Firm size is a central determinant of financial reporting behaviour within both Agency Theory and Positive Accounting Theory (PAT). From an agency perspective, larger firms are subject to greater scrutiny from regulators, analysts, and investors, which increases monitoring intensity and reduces information asymmetry. Enhanced monitoring constrains managerial opportunism and promotes higher-quality financial reporting. In addition, larger firms typically possess more sophisticated internal control systems and governance structures, which further strengthen reporting integrity. From the standpoint of PAT, the political cost hypothesis suggests that larger firms are more visible and, therefore, more likely to attract regulatory attention and public scrutiny. This creates incentives for managers to adopt more transparent and conservative reporting practices to avoid political costs. However, an alternative view posits that organisational complexity in large firms may increase information opacity and create opportunities for earnings management. Therefore, this divergence indicates that firm size operates through competing channels. That is, while it enhances monitoring and transparency, it may simultaneously increase managerial discretion. The net effect is therefore context-dependent, particularly in emerging markets where institutional enforcement varies. Owing to the above submission, this study hypothesizes that:

H01: Firm size has no positive significant effect on the financial reporting quality of quoted oil marketing companies in Nigeria.

2.2.2 Firm Age and Financial Reporting Quality 

The effect of firm age on FRQ is less theoretically settled and empirically inconsistent. On one hand, older firms are expected to exhibit higher reporting quality due to accumulated experience, established reporting systems, and reputational concerns. On the other hand, organisational inertia and entrenched managerial practices may reduce transparency over time. Empirical findings reflect this ambiguity, with studies reporting positive, negative, and insignificant relationships. This suggests that firm age may not exert a direct effect on FRQ but instead interacts with other governance and organisational factors. Ayuba et al. (2024) and Attah et al. (2024), investigated the influence of firm characteristics on the timeliness of financial reporting among quoted oil and gas companies in Nigeria from 2011 to 2020. A sample of eight (8) companies was selected. The study employed an ex post facto design. The multiple regression analysis reveals that firm age has a significant impact on the timeliness of financial reporting. The study concludes that firm attributes, particularly firm age, serve as effective predictors of financial reporting timeliness. Bello et al. (2024) investigated the moderating role of firm size in the relationship between board financial expertise and the FRQ of listed oil and gas companies in Nigeria from 2003 to 2022. The findings regarding board expertise, moderated by firm size, reveal a negative and significant impact on the FRQ of listed oil and gas companies in Nigeria. 

From an agency perspective, reputational concerns increase with firm age, thereby incentivizing transparent reporting. However, an alternative argument suggests that older firms may develop organisational inertia and entrenched managerial practices that reduce responsiveness to evolving reporting standards and weaken transparency. Furthermore, long-standing relationships between managers and stakeholders may reduce external monitoring intensity. Given these competing effects, the influence of firm age on FRQ is theoretically ambiguous. Nevertheless, in regulated industries where compliance requirements persist over time, the experience and institutionalization effects are expected to dominate. Owing to the above, this study hypothesizes that:

H02: Firm age has no positive significant effect on the financial reporting quality of quoted oil marketing companies in Nigeria.

2.2.3 Firm Leverage and Financial Reporting Quality 

The relationship between leverage and FRQ is theoretically grounded in the debt covenant hypothesis of PAT, which predicts that firms with higher leverage are subject to increased monitoring by creditors. Empirical findings generally support a positive association, suggesting that debt acts as a disciplining mechanism that constrains managerial opportunism and enhances reporting quality. Shehu (2023) analysed the monitoring characteristics and financial reporting quality of publicly listed manufacturing firms in Nigeria. A sample of 32 firms was selected from a total population of 59 for the study. Multiple regression analysis was used, and it revealed a positive and significant correlation between leverage and earnings quality. However, some studies document a negative relationship, arguing that highly leveraged firms may engage in income-increasing earnings management to avoid covenant violations. This conflicting evidence reflects a tension between monitoring effects and opportunistic incentives. In environments with weak enforcement, the opportunistic effect may dominate, whereas in more regulated contexts, monitoring effects tend to prevail. 

The relationship between leverage and financial reporting quality is theoretically grounded in the debt covenant hypothesis of PAT. Firms with higher leverage are subject to closer monitoring by creditors, who demand timely and reliable financial information to assess default risk. This external monitoring mechanism is expected to constrain managerial discretion and improve reporting quality. From an agency perspective, debt serves as a governance mechanism that disciplines managers by limiting free cash flow and imposing contractual obligations. These constraints reduce the scope for opportunistic reporting behaviour. However, an opposing argument suggests that highly leveraged firms may engage in income-increasing earnings management to avoid breaching debt covenants, thereby reducing reporting quality. The net effect of leverage on financial reporting quality depends on whether monitoring incentives outweigh opportunistic behaviour. In contexts where creditor oversight is effective, the monitoring effect is expected to dominate. Therefore, this study hypothesizes that:

H03: Firm leverage has no positive significant effect on the financial reporting quality of quoted oil marketing companies in Nigeria.

2.2.4 Profitability (ROA) and Financial Reporting Quality

Profitability is often associated with higher reporting quality, as well-performing firms have less incentive to manipulate earnings and are more inclined to signal their performance through transparent reporting. Empirical studies frequently find a positive relationship between profitability and FRQ, consistent with signalling theory. Nonetheless, counter-evidence suggests that highly profitable firms may still engage in earnings management to smooth income or meet market expectations. This indicates that profitability does not uniformly constrain opportunistic behaviour but may instead alter the form and direction of reporting incentives. Thus, the relationship between profitability and FRQ is conditional on managerial objectives and external pressures. 

According to signalling theory, highly profitable firms have incentives to disclose high-quality financial information to differentiate themselves from non-performing firms and to reduce information asymmetry. This aligns with Agency Theory, which suggests that profitable firms face lower pressure to manipulate earnings and are more likely to provide transparent reports. Conversely, PAT suggests that managers of highly profitable firms may engage in income smoothing or downward earnings management to avoid political costs or maintain stable performance trends. Despite this possibility, empirical evidence generally indicates that profitable firms are more likely to produce high-quality financial reports due to reputational concerns and market discipline. Given the dominance of signalling and reputational incentives, profitability is expected to positively influence financial reporting quality. Owing to this, the study hypothesizes that:

H04: Firm Profitability has no significant effect on the financial reporting quality of quoted oil and gas firms in Nigeria.

2.3 Theoretical Framework 

To provide the theoretical support for this research, the study considers the Positive Accounting Theory and the Agency Theory. The relationship between firm attributes and FRQ is best understood through the lens of Agency Theory and Positive Accounting Theory (PAT). Agency Theory posits that the separation of ownership and control creates information asymmetry and moral hazard, thereby incentivising managers to engage in opportunistic reporting behaviour (Omoro et al., 2015). Jensen and Meckling (1976) argue that the separation of ownership and control creates agency conflicts, which may lead to opportunistic financial reporting unless effective monitoring mechanisms are in place. Within this framework, firm attributes such as size, leverage, and profitability act as governance mechanisms that influence managerial behaviour.

PAT further explains that managerial accounting choices are shaped by contractual and political incentives (Watts & Zimmerman, 1986). The debt covenant hypothesis predicts that highly leveraged firms face stricter monitoring, while the political cost hypothesis suggests that larger firms adopt more transparent reporting practices to mitigate regulatory scrutiny. Cross-country evidence by Leuz et al. (2003) and Ball et al. (2003) further demonstrates that institutional and firm-level incentives jointly determine reporting quality, reinforcing the importance of firm-specific characteristics in shaping financial reporting outcomes. To elucidate managerial choices of accounting methods through the lens of self-interest and stakeholder relationships, PAT proposes three hypotheses: the bonus hypothesis, the contractual motivation hypothesis, and the political motivation hypothesis. Bonus plans and compensation benefits for managers often serve as motivations for the adoption of specific accounting procedures and practices. Bonuses and other compensational benefits are contingent upon generated earnings; therefore, managers are likely to support policies that enhance income. The contractual motivation hypothesis, also known as the debt covenant hypothesis, posits that firms with high leverage, nearing the point of failure to meet obligations, may adopt accounting policies that facilitate the transfer of future earnings to the current period to satisfy contractual requirements. The political motivation hypothesis suggests that firms facing intense political or public scrutiny are inclined to manage earnings downward. The PAT theory is pertinent to this study as it elucidates the motivations behind management's financial statements.


III. METHODOLOGY

Oil marketing companies were selected due to the limited research available on FRQ within this sector in Nigeria. The research employed secondary data sources. This study employed panel multiple regressions to examine the relationship between financial reporting quality, as indicated by audit report lag, and firm attributes, including firm age, firm size, leverage, and profitability.

The initial hypothesis of the study proposed that a firm's age has no significant impact on the financial reporting quality of oil marketing companies in Nigeria. The findings suggest a positive yet statistically insignificant correlation between firm age and the FRQ of the firms analysed. This suggests that a unit increase in firm age does not significantly impact FRQ, assuming that firm size, profitability, and leverage remain constant.

The second hypothesis of the study posits that firm size has no significant impact on the FRQ of oil marketing companies in Nigeria. The study's findings (β = 0.0660626, p-value < 0.05) demonstrate a positive and statistically significant correlation between firm size and the FRQ of the analysed firms. A unit change in firm size significantly enhances the quality of FRQ, provided that leverage, profitability, and firm age are held constant. The study therefore rejects the second null hypothesis (H02).

The third hypothesis posits that leverage has no significant impact on the FRQ of quoted oil marketing companies in Nigeria. The study results reveal a coefficient value of 0.1761087 and a p-value of 0.049, which exceeds the threshold of 0.05. The result demonstrates that leverage has a positive and significant impact on the FRQ of oil marketing companies in Nigeria. This suggests that an increase in leverage improves FRQ by approximately 18%, assuming that firm size, profitability, and firm age are held constant. The research findings do not support the third hypothesis (H03).

The fourth hypothesis asserts that ROA does not have a significant impact on the FRQ of oil marketing companies in Nigeria. The findings demonstrate a significant positive correlation between ROA and FRQ (β = 1.537043, p-value = 0.001), which is below 0.05. A unit change in ROA enhances FRQ of the sampled firms, controlling for firm size, leverage, and firm age.

  • Conclusion 

The results demonstrate a notable positive correlation between firm-specific characteristics and the FRQ among listed oil marketing companies in Nigeria. Standardised regression analysis indicates that firm size has a positive effect on the FRQ. The research concluded that ROA has a positive and significant influence on the FRQ of oil marketing companies in Nigeria.

  • Recommendation 

The study recommended that the quoted firms consider increasing their assets, as larger firms possess more resources for preparing high-quality financial reports. Similarly, the study recommends a combination of debt and equity, as a high debt-to-assets ratio improves FRQ. Lastly, companies are encouraged to pursue profit, as this incentivises the provision of high-quality financial accounts and reports. This can facilitate the transmission of positive signals to the market, thereby attracting potential investors and enhancing market performance.
